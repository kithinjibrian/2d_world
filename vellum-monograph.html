Vellum — A Natural History of the Plane Frontispiece

Vellum

A natural history of a world with two dimensions, its single star, and the seven surviving kinds of life that hold it.

Everything in this book follows from one fact. Vellum has length and height, and nothing else. There is no sideways. No creature has ever gone around another; no root has ever spread beneath a rival; no eye has ever been worth the trouble of growing.

What follows is arranged as a survey: the sky first, then the ground, then the rules a body must obey here, then the seven kinds of life that obey them.

The system of Kell

A star that nothing can leave.

Gravity spreads its influence over a circle here rather than a sphere, so it thins with distance rather than with the square of distance. The consequence is severe and it shapes everything above the atmosphere: Vellum's system has no escape velocity. There is no speed at which an object is finally free. Fling a stone hard enough and it will take a hundred thousand years to come back, but it will come back.

A year on Vellum runs 611 days. But the point of the orbit at which the planet passes closest to Kell creeps forward each circuit, so the warm part of the year arrives a little later every time and works its way round the calendar over roughly nine centuries. There has never been a reason to keep a calendar. Life here keys to the day, to the tide of the storms, and to hunger — never to a season.

The consequence nobody survives

Since nothing escapes, nothing is ever swept clean. Every fragment thrown off in every collision since the system formed is still in the system, on some slowly turning rosette, and sooner or later every rosette crosses Vellum's. The planet is struck hard on a cycle of roughly forty million years.

This is the metronome of Vellum's biology. Its history is not a slow accumulation but a series of floods and prunings, and every era in Plate IX begins with a sky full of debris and ends the same way.

The planet

A closed line, an atmosphere in layers, and no moon.

Vellum is a disc. Its surface is therefore a closed curve — a single continuous line 38,400 kilometres around, with no edge and no far side. Walk far enough in one direction and you arrive behind yourself. This is not a curiosity; it is the reason a barrier here is so serious. A wall across the line does not divide a landscape into two regions. It divides the world into two arcs, and there is no third way round.

Storms that outlive the things that live in them

In an atmosphere of two dimensions, turbulence runs backwards from what one might expect. Small eddies merge into larger ones instead of shredding into heat. The result is that Vellum's weather consolidates: a handful of vast rotating systems, each thousands of kilometres across, persisting for centuries. Sailors would have named them. Instead the Driftbladders navigate by them, and a bladder born inside a storm will very likely die inside the same one.

Three laws of the plane

Nearly everything else in this book is a consequence of these.

The first law and its three loopholes

Two animals standing on the ground are in a fixed order, and that order holds until one of them dies. It is impossible to overstate how much of Vellum's ecology this single fact produces: the pincer hunt, the impassable armoured grazer, the unavoidable trap, populations that speciate simply because a mountain stands between them.

Three lineages found a way round it, and each pays differently. Skimmers go over, and pay in energy. Springhoppers go over briefly, and pay by announcing their landing point the instant they leave the ground. Ridgeworms go under, and pay in speed and in the visible furrow they leave behind. Nothing else in the world can get past anything.

A wall on Vellum is not an obstacle. It is a border, and the two sides of it become two worlds within a hundred thousand years.

Water

Rivers that cannot branch, and lakes that are islands.

Water runs downhill along the surface line, and that is all it can do. A tributary would have to arrive from the side, and there is no side. Vellum therefore has no river networks, no confluences and no deltas — only single unbranched runs of water descending from high ground into whatever basin lies below.

The same reversed turbulence that consolidates the storms also refuses to stir a lake. Energy will not travel down into small eddies, so deep water never turns over. Below a certain depth every basin is dead, and the living part of a lake is a band near the surface — wide, but thin.

And a fish cannot leave. There is no flood route, no drainage web, no chance passage from one basin to another. A population washed into a basin is sealed there for the rest of its existence. This is why the Sillfish of Plate XIII is not one animal but eleven hundred of them, each confined to a single body of water, most differing from their nearest relatives by traits no one else will ever see.

What a body may be

Four things anatomy cannot do here, and the workarounds every animal shares.

In two dimensions, any closed ring of tissue severs whatever lies inside it from everything outside. There is no route over or under a loop. This single topological fact rules out most of the organs found in a three-dimensional animal, and every creature in this book carries the same set of compromises because there were no others available.

The four prohibitions

  • No through-gut. Every animal eats in batches: swallow, seal, digest, purge. This alone rules out the continuous soil-processing detritivores that dominate ground ecology elsewhere.
  • No closed circulation. A circuit of vessels would isolate everything it encircled. Blood moves through blind branching trees, driven by peristalsis, and returns the way it came.
  • No focusing eye. A lens across a pupil closes the ring and cuts the retina off from the body entirely. Vision was stranded at the open-pit stage and abandoned. See Plate VII.
  • No wheel, no axle, no rotating joint. A hub inside a housing cannot be connected to anything. Every mechanism on Vellum oscillates.

A fifth prohibition applies to engineering rather than anatomy, and it is the reason no burrowing lineage here ever built a colony: a tunnel's roof is a beam, and a beam can only span so far, but a support pillar would seal the tunnel in two. There is no way to prop a long tunnel open. Vellum has burrows. It has never had a warren.

Senses

A world of sound, and one blind spot everything shares.

No animal on Vellum can see. Three separate barriers stopped vision before it started: an aperture here is a line rather than a disc, so an eye gathers almost no light; a lens cannot be sealed into place without severing the retina; and in two dimensions a sharp flash does not stay sharp — waves develop a trailing echo as they travel, so every image would arrive smeared by its own afterglow. Vision was never worth the tissue.

Ground vibration is the finer instrument. A seismic pulse travelling along a line has nowhere to spread, so it fades only by friction. A Ridgeworm can feel a Springhopper land four kilometres away. What it cannot do is tell it anything except left or right — but on a one-dimensional surface that is the complete answer, and the timing gives the distance.

What survives of light-sensitivity is not vision but a patch: a few cells reporting brightness and nothing more. It is enough to find the sun, enough to know depth in water, and — because a shadow here is absolute — enough to feel the instant something passes overhead. That single patch is the only warning a ground animal ever gets of a Skimmer diving on it.

What a blind world sounds like

Everything that colour does elsewhere, sound does here. There is no camouflage on Vellum, no warning stripe, no display plumage, no flower. Courtship is song. Territory is a drumbeat held against a rival's. Mimicry is acoustic: the small and harmless Ridgeworm of the eastern arc produces, when alarmed, a passable imitation of a Furrowsnake's approach. The world is drab and extremely loud.

Ways of moving

Five gaits, one of which had to be invented twice.

Rotation is unavailable, so everything that moves on Vellum does so by oscillating. The ancestral motion is a travelling wave passed down the body — peristalsis in soil, undulation in water, and, with the addition of a stiffened blade at the rear, flight. All three are the same nervous pattern running in different media, which is why the Skimmer and the Sillfish are closer cousins than either looks.

The hop

Springhoppers stand on two legs, one fore and one aft — never abreast, since there is no abreast. Both legs fire together and the launch is aimed not by turning the body but by pushing harder with one than the other. The animal is symmetrical end to end and never needs to turn round, which matters more than it sounds: pitching a body through half a circle takes two seconds here, and two seconds is usually the whole hunt.

The float

The last gait is not a gait at all. A Driftbladder holds a bag of hydrogen and squeezes it, and the change in displaced air lifts or drops it. Every journey it ever makes is made by choosing a wind. It has no propulsive organ of any kind.

Deep time

Six eras, each opened and closed by the sky.

The Warm Line

Life begins in the Deepsill, in the thin band of water that is neither frozen at the surface nor stagnant below. It stays there for nearly two billion years, because the first great problem is not competition but plumbing.

The One Mouth

Every early lineage that tried to run a channel through itself split itself in half and died. The survivors of that long failure are the ancestors of everything alive: single-apertured animals with blind branching guts and blind branching blood. It is the most consequential event in Vellum's history and it left no fossils worth the name.

The Ridge

The first colonists of the surface are burrowers, arriving not by walking out of the sea but by tunnelling along the wet margin until they were, without noticing, ashore. The Ridgeworm is barely changed from them.

The Long Queue

The surface fills, and the first law begins to bite. Within forty million years the ground fauna has sorted itself into the pattern it still holds: a very few enormous armoured grazers that nothing can pass or kill, and a great many small things living in the gaps between them. Mid-sized ground carnivores appear four separate times and fail four separate times. There is simply no way to catch anything on a line that can also outrun you.

The Ascent

A basin fish with an unusually large swim bladder washes over a sill, survives, and its descendants discover that the same organ works in air. Flight is easy on Vellum — a wing has no tips, so it loses nothing at its edges — and within a few million years the sky holds more biomass than the ground. Both the Skimmers and the Driftbladders descend from that one stranded fish.

The Curtain

The most recent era begins when a Driftbladder lineage stops chasing and starts hanging. The tendril curtain closes the airspace absolutely; more importantly the bladders spread photosynthetic membrane and take the sunlight before it ever reaches the ground. Every tall plant on Vellum died in the eleven million years that followed. The ground flora that survived is the low mat we have now, and the sky is where the world's energy is captured.

Spreadturf

The mat. Not a plant so much as a surface condition.

Height14–31 cm
RangeContinuous; the entire habitable surface
ReproductionSpores carried by wind; no flower, no pollinator
Eaten byRidgeworm, and the great grazers

Spreadturf is not a species but a habit — several hundred forms so similar that no distinction has ever been drawn. Every one of them is short, and the reason is structural. A stem here is a flat strip. It has no tube, no triangulated core, nothing to resist bending, so its stiffness rises far more slowly with height than its weight does. A Spreadturf that reached a metre would buckle under itself and snap in the first storm to cross it.

This is fortunate, and not only for the animals. A plant that could grow tall on Vellum would cast a total shadow on everything behind it, and light competition here has no second place. The forests that once existed, in the age before the Driftbladders, were not forests at all — they were single enormous stalks standing kilometres apart, each in the centre of a dead zone of its own making.

What ended them was not the animals. It was the canopy that formed above them, which took the light first.

Ridgeworm

The oldest body plan still in use, and the only thing that goes under.

Length6–40 cm
MediumSoil, to about 1 m depth
SensesGround vibration only
DietSpreadturf roots, carrion, small prey — swallowed whole
Eaten byFurrowsnake, Springhopper

The Ridgeworm is very nearly unchanged from the animals that first came ashore. It moves by the ancestral wave — anchor the rear, extend, anchor the front, contract — and it is the last common gait on Vellum that requires no limb, no blade and no bag.

It cannot, however, eat its way through soil the way a burrower elsewhere might. With a single aperture doing both jobs it must swallow discrete items, seal, and digest before it can feed again. So it does not process the ground; it wedges through it, hunting by the vibration of things moving above, and surfacing to take them.

Its burrowing leaves a raised furrow along the surface that persists for days. This is the Ridgeworm's one great vulnerability and the reason Furrowsnakes exist at all: a hunter that can read the ridge knows precisely where its prey is, how deep, and how long ago it passed.

Furrowsnake

The only successful ground carnivore, and it succeeds by not staying on the ground.

Length1.4–3.1 m
SensesGround vibration, exceptionally acute
DietRidgeworms, fallen Springhoppers, carrion
HuntingFurrow-reading; pincer, in pairs

Four times in Vellum's history a mid-sized ground carnivore has evolved and four times it has starved. The arithmetic is unforgiving: on a line there is no ambush from an unexpected angle, no stealth, no cutting off an escape. Prey knows exactly where you are from your footfalls, and a chase reduces to whichever animal is faster. If the hunter is faster it exterminates its prey; if it is not, it never eats.

The Furrowsnake solves this by hunting something that cannot run: an animal already committed to a tunnel, whose position is advertised on the surface for days afterward. It does not chase. It reads.

It also hunts in pairs, and the pair does not need to coordinate. Two Furrowsnakes approaching from opposite directions have already won — there is no third direction. The difficulty is entirely in getting to opposite sides, which is why a hunting pair may travel apart for a week before converging on a stretch of ground between them.

Sillfish

Eleven hundred species, none of which have ever met.

MediumThe upper band of any standing water

BuoyancySwim bladder — the organ that later reached the sky

Water restores what the ground took away. A basin is a genuine two-dimensional region, so a Sillfish can pass another, circle it, rise above it, dive beneath it. All the manoeuvring that ground animals have never possessed is ordinary here, and the fins that would elsewhere control roll and yaw are simply absent — there is no roll and no yaw to control. A Sillfish has a body, a blade, and one small trim surface.

But a basin is also a prison. There is no drainage network on Vellum, no flood connection, no route between one body of water and the next except a one-way spill over the lip. Every basin has been sealed since it filled, and every population inside has been evolving alone for as long as it has existed. The catalogue currently stands at eleven hundred and six species. Most are separated from their nearest relative by a ridge of dry ground a few hundred metres wide, and none has ever crossed it.

One did, once. Its descendants are on Plates XIV, XV and XVI.

Skimmer

The whole animal is the wing.

Length18 cm – 1.6 m
SensesEcholocation; a dorsal light patch
DietAirborne prey, Springhoppers taken mid-hop, Driftbladder tissue
ControlsBody camber and tail pitch. Nothing else exists to control

Flight is cheap on Vellum. A wing here has no tips for pressure to spill around, so the drag that dominates flight elsewhere simply does not arise, and a Skimmer at cruise is spending energy on almost nothing but the friction of air along its skin. Flight has evolved independently three times, which tells you how low the barrier is.

Control is correspondingly simple. There is no roll, no yaw, no banking; only pitch. The Skimmer steers by arching its back, and its tail blade — a stiff flat surface beating up and down, exactly like the Sillfish's from which it descends — provides thrust on both halves of the stroke. Its trailing edge must stay sharp. A Skimmer with a damaged tail does not lose speed; it loses lift, and it does not fly again.

Two habits worth noting

The first is the low cruise. Near the ground, air trapped between the animal and the surface cannot escape sideways, and the lift benefit is far larger than it would be elsewhere. Whole lineages of Skimmer never climb at all, running the contour of the land a body-length up, for almost nothing.

The second is that Skimmers never fly in file. A wake here does not dissipate — the vortices merge and grow and hang in the air for many minutes — so following another animal directly is dangerous, and every flock on Vellum is stacked by altitude rather than strung out in a line. It also means a Skimmer can track another animal's trail through empty air long after it has gone.

Springhopper

The ground's apex predator is a child.

Length40–90 cm
SensesGround vibration and echolocation — the only animal with both
DietRidgeworms, stranded Sillfish, small Skimmers, carrion
Hop2.4 body lengths horizontal; 1.9 m vertical clearance
StatusJuvenile stage of the Driftbladder

For most of the study of Vellum the Springhopper was classified as its own animal, and the error is understandable: nothing about it resembles what it becomes. It is compact, armoured at both ends, sprung, and aggressive. It is also the only creature on the ground that can get past another, and it uses that almost exclusively for killing — a hop that ends on top of something smaller is a strike delivered with the animal's entire weight.

Its two senses tell the story of its life. Ground vibration is for hunting: it feels a Ridgeworm surfacing from kilometres off and arrives in three hops. Echolocation is for the sky, and it is not for finding food. It is for finding shadow — specifically, for knowing whether there is a Driftbladder curtain overhead before it commits to a leap.

The commonest cause of death in a Springhopper is being caught by an adult of its own species.

The curtain does not distinguish. A Springhopper that begins its metamorphosis beneath a hanging adult will inflate directly into the tendrils of something that may well be its parent. The behaviour that results is one of the strangest on Vellum: in the weeks before it changes, a Springhopper stops hunting and begins to travel, hopping in one direction along the line, sounding the sky over and over, looking for a stretch of open air. Only when it finds one does it stop and begin to swell.

Driftbladder

The largest living thing on Vellum, and the least able to go anywhere.

SpanBag 4–11 m; curtain to 60 m

PropulsionNone
SensesEcholocation; light patches on the membrane
DietSunlight, and whatever the curtain takes
Lifespan80–140 years

Everything a Driftbladder does, it does by changing how much air it displaces. A ring of muscle around the bag contracts and it sinks; relaxes and it rises. It loses no gas doing this, so it can repeat it forever, and the manoeuvre takes seconds. That single control is its entire behavioural repertoire — its travel, its hunting, its escape and its courtship.

Travel works because the winds run counter at different heights. A bladder that wants to go one way rises until it finds a layer going that way. It follows that a Driftbladder's competence is not physical but geographic: it must know the circulation of the storm it lives in, at every altitude, and it must remember how that circulation shifts. Individuals that know their storm outlive drifters by decades. Whatever passes for intelligence on Vellum is here, in an animal that cannot move under its own power.

The curtain

It does not chase, because nothing needs to. Hanging tendrils from the body down toward the ground seals the airspace absolutely — there is no way round a curtain, only over it or through the gap beneath. The hunt consists of timing a descent so that the gap closes as something arrives. Two bladders at different heights can shut the sky entirely, and a pair that does so over a mountain pass will, within a few generations, have split the fauna on either side into different species.

Sunlight and the deal it strikes

The membrane spread above the bag is the highest surface on the world and is therefore never shaded. It supplies carbon and energy but no minerals — nitrogen, phosphorus and metals are in the ground, and an adult never touches the ground. This is the whole reason for the curtain, and the whole reason for the Springhopper: the juvenile spends years on the surface eating meat and hoarding minerals, and the adult spends a century in the sky spending them.

Its only predators come from above, where it cannot defend itself. A large Skimmer that climbs and dives can open a bag, and a bladder that loses its gas has a very long time to think about it on the way down.

The web

Where Vellum's energy goes.

Two things about this diagram are worth dwelling on. The first is the loop running from the Driftbladder down to the Springhopper: that arrow is the curtain, and it means the largest animal on Vellum is a significant predator of its own young. It is not an accident of an overlapping niche. It is the mechanism that forces juveniles to disperse before they metamorphose, and it is very likely why Springhoppers travel at all.

The second is the arrow returning from the Skimmer to the Driftbladder. Everything else in this web feeds upward. That one arrow is the only thing keeping the sky in balance — remove the diving Skimmers and the bladders would shade the surface to death within a few million years, as they nearly did once already at the beginning of the present era.

What never happened

A short list of ordinary things this world has never contained.

  • Colour. Nothing has ever seen, so nothing has ever needed to be seen. No camouflage, no warning stripes, no mimicry by appearance, no flowers.
  • The wheel. Not merely undiscovered — impossible. A hub inside a housing cannot be joined to anything.
  • Tunnel networks. A burrow cannot be propped without being sealed, so no colony, no hive, no warren has ever been dug.
  • The confluence. No river has ever joined another river. No delta has ever formed.
  • Escape. Nothing thrown from Vellum has ever left the system, which is why the sky keeps falling on it.
  • Passing. On the ground, in four billion years, nothing has ever gone around anything.
  • Night as a refuge. Since no predator ever hunted by light, darkness changed nothing. The only creature for which night matters is the Driftbladder, whose gas cools and sinks after sunset.
  • Formation flight, schooling, the herd. A wake in this air does not disperse. To follow directly behind another animal is to fly into a standing wall of turning air.
Everything here is a profile. If a thing cannot be drawn as a single closed outline — if any part of it must be seen from the front — then it has never existed.

A note on the survey

Seven kinds of life are described here and that is very nearly all of them. Vellum has no equivalent of an insect radiation, no reef, no grass-and-grazer arms race with a hundred players. The plane is a narrow place: it offers a line to stand on, a band of air, a band of water, and a shallow crust. There is not much room to be different in.

What it offers instead is severity. Every rule is absolute here — a shadow takes all the light, an obstruction stops everything, a wall makes two worlds. Nothing is a matter of degree. That is what makes a world of two dimensions worth the trouble of imagining: not that it is stranger than ours, but that it is so much less forgiving of the same physics.
